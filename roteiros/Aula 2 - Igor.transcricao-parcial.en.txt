I think if you teach surgeons appropriately how to do this, of course, I think surgeons can close this, and having the appropriate tools, I do think using the robot is something of an advantage in these cases, and being able to reduce the complex closure here as opposed to doing a laparostomy. This is technically quite challenging to perform.

What study would you need to look at posterior? I think you would need before and after CT scans, at least after CT scan, specifically looking at the posterior layer. There is some finding, sometimes when the posterior layer comes apart, which are very obvious on CT scan, that we can certainly talk about. But that type of study would settle it. And I would get those CT scans about six months afterwards and as well as two years afterwards. In my opinion, once the mesh sets in place and kind of holds everything together, long term, that posterior layer is unlikely to come apart or things to slip in there anymore, because again, the mesh integrates into the posterior layer and the underside of the rectus abdominis muscle. Most of those failures, or if not all of those failures, happen early postoperative period, if not immediately during the wake-up. That is an important thing to consider. And long term, if, say, you get a CT scan about eight weeks after surgery in every case like that, it is my belief that it is unlikely to come apart and it is unlikely to develop an incisional hernia, especially if you really address the whole retrorectal space, whether from subxiphoid space all the way down to the space of Retzius, as close to posterior sheath. It is unlikely at that point to come apart in the long term. That has been my experience.
